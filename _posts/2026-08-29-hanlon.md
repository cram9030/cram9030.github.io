At work over the years I've found myself repeatadly quoting Hanlon's Razor

> Never attribute to malice that which is adequately explained by stupidity.

in response to a variety of things from why advisaries were not in scope for detect and avoid manuevers but uncoperative aircraft were to explaining the behaviors of certain managers behavior within our organization. The origin of the modern quote comes from the book [Murphy's Law Book Two: More Reasons Why Things Go Wrong](https://archive.org/details/murphyslawbooktw00bloc/page/n99/mode/2up) which is a humorous collection of axioms, conundrums and corollaries. As a book it is really meant to be a collection of pithy cynical comments that are relatable and have some face validity. This particular razor was supposibly submitted by Robert Hanlon but the concept predates him. [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Hanlons-razor) has an excellent history if you are interested in learning more about the previous incarnations.

My personal variation of it is a little different

> Never attribute to malice that which is adequtely explained by *incompetence*. 

In a lot of ways these are fairly close statements because a stupid person is often going to be incompetent but incompetence is a little more narrowly tuned to a specific purpose as opposed to making a broad statement about a persons capabilities. I think this is important because everyone is incompetent in certain areas. Maybe we are just new to the task at hand and lack the experience to currently be competent or maybe it is just a task we are bad at but we are good at other things. Mostly when I think of the difference though I tend to think of things that make us briefly incompetent. We know for example time pressure or exhaustion are really good ways to turn a compentent person into an incompetent one. 

I am not the first person to make a similar observation. While I can't say that I've read his book **The Failure of Risk Management: Why It’s Broken and How to Fix It** (2009) Douglas W. Hubbard's version of Hanlon's razor from that book is

> Never attribute to malice or stupidity that which can be explained by moderately rational individuals following incentives in a complex system of interactions.

It is a bit more of mouthful but I think it speaks to another reason for incompetence which is misaligned incentives. Examples of this at companies are typically pretty easy to find, assessment on the number of closed tickets incentivising "Could not reproduce" responses or more recently token maxxing running up AI bills when they were intended to increase responsiveness and encourage LLM adoption respectively. To the point about complex systems of interactions though I have found these are often tied to how things get funded. When I was at NASA there was always a push to get the centers to collaborate with each other on projects but this ran into a zero sum game of financing where if one center got an award or a porition of funding it neccesarily meant that the other would not have it and there was nothing they could do to replace that funding putting members of their contracting team at risk. This became even more exaserbated when it came to the California centers like NASA Ames where the the cost of living adjustments made the indivduals more expensive so some funders in programs would prefer to balance towards other centers where the labor dollars were more efficent.
