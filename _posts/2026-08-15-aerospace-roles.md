I’ve been a reader of the [Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/) for a few years now and really appreciate the clear articulation of what can be very confusing roles and expectations that come with them, mostly in Big Tech and scale-ups. I found the posts like [Engineering Career Paths](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/engineering-career-paths), [Engineering Leadership Skill Set Overlaps](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/engineering-leadership-skillset-overlaps), and [What TPMs Do and What Software Engineers Can Learn from Them](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/what-tpms-do?utm_source=publication-search) really helpful in framing and explaining those roles and, often, the pitfalls associated with them, without the corporate language or histrionics of Reddit. It inspired me to search for the same sort of things in aerospace. While I am sure it exists somewhere, I wasn’t able to find an analog that was sufficiently motivating for me to create one myself.

The motivation comes from two experiences. First, I have often found myself over the last few years, since I left NASA and went into industry, explaining (and sometimes having explained to me) what certain roles were and why they existed in an aerospace company. Working at electric Vertical Takeoff and Landing (eVTOL) companies, they have a very healthy mix of different industries coming together, with aerospace, electric automobile, and tech-focused software as the primary intersections. Of these fields, aerospace is the most well-established, and a large chunk of why it is this way comes from NASA and the space race really kicking off the industry. As such, the roles and organizational structure seemed like distant cousins to me, coming from NASA, whereas others from different industries were in a confused state of culture shock. It was really common for me to sit there hearing their complaints and then say “well that’s not really their focus,” only to have the person complaining stop and ask what I meant. Which then often led to questions of whether that’s not their focus, then what is, and who is focusing on the problem, which is their current headache?

The second motivation came from my own career, where, very early on, I thought I’d be a good systems engineer and told people as much. In my mind, I thought of a systems engineer as having an almost T-shaped career progression, where once I had my PhD, that would be my deep knowledge. I could branch out and become a systems engineer, allowing me to understand the whole system, then progress to a managerial role, which would expand from technical breadth to programmatic breadth. At the time, I didn’t know about the T-shaped career model; it wasn’t until many years later, while telling a story to a mentee, that they said, “Oh, you mean like the T-shaped career model?” and I looked it up. It represented a misunderstanding that really took me leaving NASA and seeing how anyone with Project or Program in their title was treated; I really started to think more about it.

I chose to keep the same general grouping as Gregely did with Strategy & Alignment, People Management, and Engineering (replacing Building Software). I tried a few different groupings, but it quickly became overly complicated and missed the point. My definitions are slightly different as well.
- **Strategy & Alignment** - Providing a clear mapping of the organization and product goals to technical execution. This can be as big as the market the company wants to target to as small as the decomposition of requirements.
- **Engineering** - Turning ideas into reality, creating things that can impact the real world, achieving the goals of the organization or product.
- **People Management** - Curating a productive, efficient team culture, making sure that the team is growing in knowledge and success, if not necessarily in headcount.

Obviously, these don’t cover everything, and not every organization uses the same titles or responsibilities, but generally, what I have seen is summarized below.

A lot is going on here, and before we get into a more detailed breakdown, it’s worth explaining some inclusions on the Venn Diagram. I included Safety and Systems Engineers but did not break down any other engineering domains. This is because both have unique responsibilities that overlap with Strategy & Alignment, regardless of seniority. I included Tech Lead, a role that often doesn’t have a specific title and is typically held by either a staff member or a distinguished engineer. It’s a distinct role nonetheless, just one that doesn’t get a title because it is typically associated with a single project, program, or engineering effort. Finally, the inclusion of the Designated Engineering Representative (DER) is fundamentally an alignment role between the organization and the FAA. They are unique to civil certification, but someone fills this role even in military projects; they are just harder to identify there.

It is also worth addressing why the Engineering Manager and Director have where they should be in people management. This is simply because their roles should be to spend the vast majority of their time managing the people in their employ, but often don’t. In my experience, Directors never venture into the Engineering bubble but spend the vast majority of their time on alignment issues. Engineering Managers, on the other hand, can vary widely across the three domains, depending on personal biases and organizational needs.

## Breaking it down
In this section, we will go over each of the roles captured above and describe each role, its focus area, the timeframe of that focus, how it is often viewed, and a quick summary of common issues associated with that role. The timeframe is an important addition, but it typically helps differentiate between types of managers who share the same focus area but differ primarily in when they focus on it. A good example is that a CTO has many of the same responsibilities as a Chief Engineer. However, a Chief Engineer is mostly focused on a single program or project. At the same time, the CTO looks at the portfolio over a long-term horizon, often no earlier than the next-generation vehicle. So the difference between the two roles is that the CTO takes a long view of many things, whereas the Chief Engineer takes a view within a single project over its lifespan.

### Tech Lead
- **Focus:** Engineering, Strategy and Alignment
- **Time Frame:** Near term (months - 2 years)
- **Description:** The tech lead is a staff engineer with the authority to make technical decisions. This is not often an explicit title, though sometimes it is. This is a person who is responsible for the system design, knows it inside and out, and will also contribute to its development. They often provide the tactical *how*, whereas the Engineering manager provides the strategic *how*.
- **How they are viewed:** Typically, when someone says "Subject Matter Expert (SME)," they mean the Tech Lead. They are viewed as the go-to person who will have the answer for their system or component.
- **Common issues:** The lead part is leadership, not in a people-management sort of activity, but about setting an example both technically and professionally. Sometimes they can fall victim to paralysis by analysis, unwilling to commit because there are still unknowns and they are being questioned. Other times, they might not trust anyone else to carry out their vision and end up taking on too much of the technical work.

### Systems Engineer
- **Focus:** Engineering, Strategy and Alignment
- **Time Frame:** Near term (months - 2 years)
- **Description:** There has been a lot of ink spilled on what a [systems engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systems_engineering) is and isn’t supposed to do. How I like to think of it is they are responsible for translating *what* a complex product is supposed to do into *what* systems and subsystems are supposed to do. This provides a very broad purview in which significant and strategic abstraction is necessary to bound and execute the product's creation, while at the same time the realities of how the components exist and come together have to be included. They are most often associated with the systems engineering process, which involves rigorous top-down decomposition of requirements and bottom-up verification of those requirements, leaving the systems engineer responsible for the entire lifecycle of a product in a way no other role outside the Chief Engineer is expected to be.
- **How they are viewed:** The first time I told a friend I thought I might want to go into systems engineering, their response was “Why? All they do is check boxes in Excel.” There are many more nuanced views, but most are close to the idea that the system engineer is someone who mindlessly follows a process.
- **Common issues:** The process of determining and decomposing what runs a serious risk of forgetting *why* it is being done. The system engineering processes were created to provide rigor and consistency to something complex enough that things were likely to be missed, but process is not the objective; it’s a means of meeting it, which can be lost and turn the exercise into a somewhat mindless one if a more holistic view is not taken.

### Safety Engineer
- **Focus:** Engineering, Strategy and Alignment
- **Time Frame:** Near term (months - 2 years)
- **Description:** Safety engineers, much like systems engineers, have a heavy involvement with Strategy and Alignment no matter how junior. This is because they are responsible for building the safety case, which decomposes the hazards of any product, identifies the allowable likelihood of them given the impact, and then assesses the architecture proposed by the Chief Engineer and their team against the established safety case. The safety case is critical for certification, so how hazards are allocated and the allowed uncertainty margins are critical to the development strategy, especially early in an unproven or nonexistent concept of operations.
- **How they are viewed:** There is often frustration that safety engineers don’t have the answers to how safe something must be. Most of their tools are for assessing an architecture, not for projecting what is needed. This leads to a lot of confusion about what a safety engineer can and cannot tell the other engineering teams. As a result, they are often the no vote after significant development time has been devoted to them, which can make them rather unpopular.
- **Common issues:** When the analysis comes back and lacks face validity, it can be a serious problem. I had an instance where we were mounting a camera on an uncrewed aircraft, and the safety analysis came back showing that the camera was more safety-critical to the operation than the propulsion system because it introduced a bunch of new failure modes for the pilot, like loss of awareness. There is little face validity to the claim that a blind pilot is safer than one who might lose awareness due to a frozen screen. The issue was that those operational failure modes had not been considered before, but putting the camera on board required them to be considered. The mirror of this problem exists as well, where any design can be pencil-whipped into being safe enough if the numbers and assumptions are cut correctly.

### Project Manager
- **Focus:** Strategy and Alignment
- **Timeframe:** Near term (1-2 years)
- **Description:** The owner of *when* things need to be done. Within a given project, they are tasked with mapping out each component's dependencies and ensuring the work is completed in the correct order to enable sequential execution. This often requires prioritizing certain tasks over others. Further task decomposition is required to prioritize blocking scope effectively. This role is more of a hallmark of a matrixed organization, where the responsibility for when things need to happen is separated from the Engineering Manager, freeing them to focus on their teams.
- **How they are viewed:** A heuristic I didn’t know existed until I left NASA is that if "Project" is in your title, engineers will assume you are an idiot who is safe to ignore, because listening to you will just add work with no value.
- **Common issues:** It's very hard to divorce *when* something is supposed to be done from *how* it is supposed to be done and *who* should do it. Most frequently, this manifests as the Project Manager not having a deep technical understanding, so they cannot identify interdependencies without the aid of the roles they should be relieving in the Tech Lead or Engineering Manager. This role is functionally reduced to a status-keeper for upper management, without delivering for those executing.

### Program Manager
- **Focus:** Strategy & Alignment, People Management
- **Timeframe:** Mid-Term (3-8 years)
- **Description:** A program is typically an aircraft, though sometimes large components that might be used across multiple aircraft can be its own program. The same engine used on multiple airframes might be its own program, and each airframe could be its own program as well. Sometimes this role will also be referred to as an [Integrated Product Team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_product_team) Lead, though that role can tend to be a little closer to a Technical Program Manager role. Functionally, in either case, it is where multiple discipline-specific projects are integrated, and the program manager is responsible for that. They tend to have project managers reporting to them. They are responsible for ensuring the program is executed effectively and determining when it needs to deliver *what* the Chief Engineer (and their team) has designed. They are also typically responsible for the budget and communication to executives. This is not normally a technical role and can be part of the [Program Management Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_management_office).
- **How the are viewed:** Most of the time, they are viewed as the human embodiment of the planning fallacy and as adding unnecessary overhead. Most of that overhead is actually intended to resolve organizational blind spots.
- **Common Issues:** The schedules tend to be top-down with when things need to be done, but the scope is often not defined well enough to have a reasonable expectation of closing.

### Technical Program Manager
- **Focus:** Strategy & Alignment
- **Timeframe:** Near term (1-2 years)
- **Description:** This is a role that is uncommon but has been growing recently, particularly in the UAV or eVTOL companies in the Bay Area. As the name implies, they are Program Managers with technical knowledge that helps them address the shortcomings that a non-technical project manager might have in determining *when* something needs to happen. One of the big differences between a Tech TPM (for a much better introduction into a Tech TPM, see the Pragmatic Engineer’s ["What TPMs do?"](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/what-tpms-do?utm_source=publication-search)) and an Aerospace TPM is that a Tech TPM determines *who* is responsible not at an individual level but at an organizational level, what group is responsible for a particular scope of work. You will be hard-pressed to find an aerospace TPM with the authority to do that; instead, that is often the purview of the director.
- **How they are viewed:** Generally, they are viewed with confusion or are just treated as a Project Manager.
- **Common issues:** As alluded to, it is a new role for aerospace. The people who fill the role are often not technical enough to deliver results that differ from those of the existing Project or Program Manager roles. They often have conflicts with the Engineering Managers and Directors, where the Directors are driving the who organizationally. However, the TPM still has responsibility but no authority, which results in them frequently seeking out individuals to close deliverables, thereby stepping on Engineering Managers in the process.

### Product Manager
- **Focus:**: Strategy and Alignment
- **Timeframe:** 5-20 years
- **Description:** Determines and drives the details of *what* the product will need to do and *why* it should exist. They need to understand the market and customers' needs. They will be the ones drafting the market requirements and objectives, which will inform the L0 (highest-level) requirements the product must meet. They will also often help adjudicate interpretations of those requirements or weigh which requirements should be changed versus kept due to technical realities.
- **How they are viewed:** Engineering teams tend to view Product Managers as not understanding the technology or what it is capable of, yet comfortable making unrealistic demands of it. In general I'd say the [view of Product Mangers in Big Tech](https://www.businessinsider.com/product-managers-rule-silicon-valley-tech-industry-programmers-jobs-ceo-2024-11) is largely echoed in Aerospace. 
- **Common issues:** There’s often a risk that every customer-facing position overindexes on customer response. Different customers have different needs, and an effective Product Manager doesn’t over-respond to one critique or another. In Aerospace, especially with its long timelines, changes in vision at the top level can set a program back significantly. In the worst case they can run the risk of becoming an example of [Brandolini's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brandolini%27s_law) where they ask a question like “Why can’t it just…?” or “This related field does…?” which are innocent enough questions but when a report has to be draft to explain why it doesn’t work here because of, physics, combinations of requirements, or regulation it turns into a drag on the other teams.

### Engineering Manager
- **Focus:** People Mangement, Strategy & Alignment
- **Timeframe:** Near-Term (1 month - 1 year)
- **Description:** Top priority is making sure that the necessary skill sets represented by their team are competent and available for tasks. They are typically tasked with determining the who and the how an activity is executed. The who is obviously the people management roles, but how is typically because they are often, or were, domain experts, so they can know what the best practices for the team are going to be and enforce them. A big part of ensuring the right skills are available is hiring and growing the team, including ensuring they are mentally and technically ready to engage.
- **How they are viewed:** In my experience, most managers are liked by their teams. This has also been borne out whenever I’ve seen HR put out surveys: the questions about individuals' direct managers are often good, even if the company as a whole is going down.
- **Common issues:** As I drew in the Venn diagram, Engineering Managers tend to spend a lot of time on Strategy & Alignment. One could argue that determining who fills a particular need fits there, but oftentimes I’ve seen Engineering Managers want to have a say in what gets done and when it should be done. Those are, in fact, the tasks of the Product Manager and, to some extent, the Program/Project Manager. A lot of time and effort that could be spent on the team instead gets dedicated to trying to push the program to view what they think is the right path and the right way. Additionally, an Engineering Manager who defends their team and sets them up for success can fairly easily come across as intransigent and not a team player. Engineering Managers who are early in their tenure might instead face the opposite issue: they accept and volunteer for too much, stunting their team's growth.

### Designated Engineering Representative
- **Focus:** Strategy and Alignment
- **Timeframe:** Mid-Term (3-8 years)
- **Description:** The [Designated Engineering Representative](https://www.faa.gov/other_visit/aviation_industry/designees_delegations/individual_designees/der), or DER, is a unique position in civil aviation. They are appointed by the FAA to essentially serve as a private representative of the FAA, either by consulting or through employment with a company. They essentially have been given the authority to confirm if you are doing things the way the FAA would want you to. They always belong to a particular engineering discipline, so they are technical people, but their role is really to ensure the company is aligned with the FAA. They will often be included in the strategy's construction as well, because certification is a necessary but not sufficient condition for a product. So they will provide strategic guidance on how a product could be certified and changes that make it more or less likely to happen. One of their other roles is translating the FAA’s documentation, which is essentially legal, into technically executable guidance.
- **How they are viewed:** There is a wide range of views that tend to split along the consultant vs company DER. Some people firmly believe company DERs are not as good as consulting ones because “Why would a DER want to make less money being part of a company?” which is a little silly because almost every role has some trade space where dependable paychecks versus quantity of the paycheck steers people one way or another. For either type of DER, engineering teams often feel frustrated as they try to follow the legal-to-technical decomposition.
- **Common issues:** This role runs the risk of turning into [Hitchens's razor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hitchens's_razor) “What can be asserted without evidence can also be dismissed without evidence,” in that in debates with technical staff they might say “The FAA won’t certify that” or “There is no way that will be acceptable to the FAA” with no evidence. They often do have evidence, and most of the time it comes in the form of anecdotes. The fact that you tend to get better responses with more frequent anecdotes from hourly consulting DERs rather than company ones also plays into their perception. This isn’t to say there is Machiavellian profit maximization, just that they will have dedicated time to the task of explaining, whereas a company DER will often have other competing responsibilities to tend to.

### Director
- **Focus:** Strategy & Alignment, People Management
- **Timeframe:** Mid-Term (3-8 years)
- **Description:** Often a step up from the Engineering Manager, they tend to have many of the same responsibilities but trend more towards *who* is needed over the mid and long term to achieve the strategic visions of the organization. They will often be responsible for identifying which functionalities do not currently exist or won’t be needed to achieve the vision laid out by the CTO. They also own *when* those functionalities need to be added or sunset.
- **How they are viewed:** While the Engineering Managers tend to be well liked by their teams, the Director is often (though not always) viewed the opposite way. If there’s a problem but it’s not your boss's fault, who is the next logical person? Why their boss, of course! Newer employees hired during an expansion don’t tend to have warm feelings, because their hiring and their team are obvious. However, the person holding the blade to your project or team will result in an outsized negative view due to loss aversion.
- **Common issues:** Level blindness, often due to an organization's structure; even when the Director tries to be available, they tend to receive only highly filtered information about the state of what they are responsible for. As a result, their view of the current state of affairs is often flawed, which, when combined with the  [planning falacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planning_fallacy), leads to ramp-ups/ramp-downs at the wrong times.

### Chief Engineer
- **Focus:** Strategy & Alignment, Engineering, People Management
- **Timeframe:** Mid-Term (3-8 years)
- **Description:** One of the roles that varies organization to organization. Oftentimes, though, they are the architect of the aircraft. They might be described as owning the systems of systems, which defines the operational behavior of the aircraft being described. As such, they are frequently the final technical say on any decision.
- **How they are viewed:** Of all the roles listed here, the Chief Engineer has the greatest impact on perception. They are more accessible than a CTO but small enough in number that the rest of the community with the same role does not overcome their actual contributions. As a result, the view can vary from legendary to politically pointed hack most often. However, it is viewed as capable if a little too focused on hitting immediate milestones.
- **Common issues:** As the final technical say on a project/program, they often become the bottleneck where the whole program narrows down to decisions made by one individual or maybe one individual and their staff.

### Chief Technology Officer
- **Focus:** Strategy & Alignment, Engineering, People Management
- **Timeframe:** Long Term (5-20+ years)
- **Description:** * Taking an assessment of where the company wants to place itself in the market in the long-term future, what portfolio of technology must be developed in-house to achieve that versus what will mature in the future outside of the company and therefore should not be invested in. This role, among the ones listed, spends the most time analyzing technical counterfactuals, assessing technology risks and opportunities in maturing but unrelated technologies that could impact the future. They often have a small direct reporting staff, but in some smaller organizations, all technical roles will report to the CTO. Research and Development is the most common group to report directly to the CTO, while engineering tends to have a different reporting structure once a company reaches a certain size.
- **How they are viewed:** You’re bosses, boss, or bosses, bosses, boss. They are often viewed as the decision makers for technical people. They are typically so far out of reach that, unless they are actively meddling, most engineers have a naively positive view of their representation at the top.
- **Common issues:** Because it is the top role, it is often seen as having the final say on technical matters. This isn’t actually true because technology is a means of impacting the world and solving problems. Deciding what problem to solve, given the constraints of time, money, and organization, isn’t something one person can do. On the flip side, because they are typically more focused on technology, they are likely to take more losses than expected in political battles. Politics wasn’t in the description, but it is certainly part of the role.

## Observations 

I find it interesting that so many unique roles are devoted to Strategy and Alignment, or at least contributing to it. Most people who look at this diagram would probably say it’s mostly an indication of too much overhead. I think that is probably true, but some of it speaks to the degree of complexity of an aerospace system. There are many stakeholders, such as the FAA, who are much more influential for civilian aircraft than regulators in other domains are. Another part of it is the risk-reward of aerospace. It is a very expensive industry with little to no way to start small. Low-cost launches, CubeSats, and UAVs have opened up lower-cost avenues in aerospace. However, launch remains expensive, so as new space companies have grown, some of the same dynamics have begun to shape their organizations. The UAV companies that have grown significantly have started seeing more program people creep in as well. However, I think it is much less than in traditional, and in particular civil, aerospace.

One common thread is that many of the issues stem from a lack of understanding, poor communication (often due to time constraints), or the planning fallacy. I tend to think of this as a grouping under [Hanlon’s razor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanlon%27s_razor), “Never attribute to malice what is adequately explained by incompetence.” Some roles tend to attract incompetence for whatever reason, which manifests as a lack of understanding, but most end up being made incompetent by various forms of pressure. There is evidence that [merely feeling rushed will reduce performance](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9506568/). While there has been considerable consideration of [pilot workload for a long time](https://hf.tc.faa.gov/publications/1983-the-measurement-of-pilot-workload/full_text.pdf), the consequences of workload and rushing in management roles seem nearly nonexistent, yet are likely to manifest many common issues. It seems that aerospace, due to the long timelines, low-frequency learning cycles, and heavy penalties for mistakes, might be especially prone to creating its own incompetence.
