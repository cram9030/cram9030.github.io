I've been a reader of the [Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/) for a few years now and really appreciated the clear articulation of the what can be very confusing roles and expections that come with them across mostly Big Tech and scale ups. I found the posts like the [Engineering Career Paths](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/engineering-career-paths), [Engineering Leadership Skill Set Overlaps](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/engineering-leadership-skillset-overlaps), and [What TPMs Do and What Software Engineers Can Learn from Them](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/what-tpms-do?utm_source=publication-search) really helpful in framing and explaining those roles and often times pitfalls associated with them, without the corporate language or histrionics of Reddit. It inspired me to search for the same sort of things in aerospace. While I am sure it exists somewhere I wasn't able to find an analog which was enough of a motivation for me to create one myself.

The motivation comes from two experiences. First I often have found myself over the last few years since I left NASA and went into industry explaining (and sometimes having explained to me) what certain roles were and why they existed in an aerospace company. Working at electric Vertical Take off and Landing (eVTOL) companies they have a very health mix of different industries coming together with aerospace, electric automobile, and tech focused software being the primary intersections. Of these fields aerospace is the most well established and a large chunk why it is this way comes from NASA and the space race really kicking off the industry. As such the roles and organization structure seemed like distant cousins to me coming from NASA where as others from different industries were in confused culture shock. It was really common for me to sit there hearing their complaints and then say "well that's not really their focus" only to have the person complaining stop and ask what I meant. Which then often lead to questions of if that's not their focus then what is and who is focusing on the problem which is their current headache?

The second motivation came from my own career where very early on I thought I'd be a good systems engineer and told people as much. In my mind I thought of a systems engineer almost as a T shaped career progression where once I had my PhD that would be my deep knowedge then I could branch out and become a systems engeer allowing me to understand the whole system then progress to a manager role which would expand from a technical breadth to a programatic one. At the time I didn't T shaped career model was it wasn't until many years later where I was telling a story to a mentee of mind that they said "Oh you mean like the T shaped career model?" and I looked it up. It represented a misunderstand that the time that it really took me leaving NASA and seeing how anyone with Project or Program in their title was treated that I really started to think more about it. 

I choose to keep the same general grouping as Gregely did with Strategy & Alignment, People Management, and Engineering (replacing Building Software). I tried a few different groupings but it quickly cascaded into the overly complicated and missing the point. My definitions are slightly different as well.
- **Strategy & Alignment** - Providing a clear mapping of the organization and product goals to technical execution. This can be as big as the market the company wants to target to as small as the decomposition of requirements. 
- **Engineering** - Turning ideas into reality. Creating things that can impact the real-world, achieving the goals the organization or product.
- **People Management** - Curating productive efficent team culture. Making sure that the team is growing in knowledge and success if not neccesarily head count.

Obviously these don't cover everything and not every organization keeps the same titles or responsibilities but generally what I have seen is summarized below.

There is a lot going on here and before we get into a more detailed break down it's worth explaining some inclusions on the Venn Diagram. I included Safety and Systems Engineers but did not break down any other domain of engineering. This is because those both have unique responsibilities that force and overlap into Strategy & Alignment no matter the senority. I included Tech Lead which is a role that often time doesn't come with a specific title but is normally occupied by either a staff or distinguished engineer. It's a distinct role none the less just one that doesn't get a title because it is typically associated with a single project, program, or engineering effort. Finally the inclusion of the Designated Engineering Representative (DER) this is fundamentally an alignment role between the organization and the FAA. They are unique to civil certification but there is someone of fills this role even if it is a military project they are just harder to identify in that case.

It is also worth addressing why Engineering Manager and Director have where they should be in people management. This is simply because their roles should be spending the vast majority of their time managing the people in their employ but often don't. In my experience Directors never venture into the Engineering bubble but spend the vast majority of their time on alignment issues. Engineering Managers on the other hand can vary widely between the intersections of the three domains depending on personal biases and organizational needs but they should be managers closer to the engineering more frequently than they actually get to be.

## Breaking it down
In this section we will go over each of the roles that were captured above and provide a description of them, their focus area, timeframe of that focus, how they are often viewe, and a quick summary of common issues found with those roles. The timeframe is an important addition but it typically speaks to differentiating different type of managers who have the same focus area but differ primarily over when they are focused on. A good example is a CTO has a lot of the same responsibilities as a Chief Engineer but a Chief Engineer is mostly focused on a single program or project while the CTO is looking at the portfolio over a long term horizon that is often no earlier than the next generation vehicle. So the difference between the two roles is that the CTO is taking a long view of many things where as the Chief Engineer is taking an view within a single project over that lifespan. 

### Chief Engineer
- **Focus:** Strategy & Alignment, Engineering, People Management
- **Timeframe:** Mid-Term (3-8 years)
- **Description:** On of the roles that varies widely organization to organization. Often times though they are the architect of the aircraft. They might be described as owning the systems of systems which defines the operational behavior of the aircraft being described. As such they are frequently the final technical say on any decision.
- **How they are viewed:** Of all of the roles listed here the Chief Engineer is the one that the individual has the largest impact on how they are seen. Being more accessible than a CTO but small enough in number that the rest of the community with the same role does not over come their actual contributions. As a result the view can vary from legenday to politically pointed hack. Most often though it is simply viewed as capable if a little to focused on hitting immediate milestones.
- **Common issues:** As the final technical say on a project they often become the bottleneck where the whole program narrows down to decisions made by one individual or maybe one individual and their staff. 

### Cheif Technology Officer
- **Focus:** Strategy & Alignment, Engineering, People Management
- **Timeframe:** Long Term (20+ years)
- **Description:** Taking an assessment of given where the company want to place itself in the market in the long term future what portfolio of technology must be developed in house to achieve that versus what will mature in the future outside of the company and there for should not be invested in. This role of all the ones list spends the most time on analyzing techical counterfactuals, assessing technology risk and opertunities of maturing but unrelated technologies which could impact the future. They often have a small direct reporting staff but in some smaller organizations all technical roles will report up into the CTO. Research and Development is the most common group to report directly into the CTO while engineering tends to have a different reporting structure once a company gets to a certain size.
- **How they are viewed:** You're bosses, boss, or bosses, bosses, boss. They are often viewed as the decision makers for technical people. They typically are so far out of reach that unless they are actively meddling most engineers have a naively positive view of their represetnation at the top. 
- **Common issues:** Due to being the top role it is often thought of as having the final say on technical matters. This isn't actually true because technology is a means to impact the world and solve a problem deciding what problem to solve with the constraints of time, money, and organization isn't something one person can do. On the flip side because they are typically more focused on technology when there is a political battle they are liable to take more losses than expected. Politics wasn't in the description but it is certainly part of the role.
