It has been 9 years since I last attended IROS 2017 in Vancouver. A lot has changed since then. While I tried to stay up to date with robotics, my day-to-day job has been mostly aerospace-related during that time, and my robotics exposure has come from friends, coworkers, and papers I was interested in. This created a pretty sizable selection bias in the quantity of certain content I'd been reading and exposed to. Having spent the week at IROS, a few things stood out to me.

1. I think the broader community is over-indexing on VLA/VLM/LLM, but I understand why. The performance and capabilities are just beyond anything else at the moment, and with new models being released constantly with different harassing options, prompting, and skills to explore and develop, there is a lot of research still to be had. That being said, even taking that into account, it felt overrepresented in talks and on the floor. It seemed to come at the cost of a lot of good research that had been developing in a slightly slower lane but still very rapidly over the past five or so years, which still has potential, and it left me feeling like there needed to be a little more diversity in the content.
2. I'd like to see more analysis on the benefits of open vocabulary for segmentation, particularly for navigation. The open vocabulary brings a lot of flexibility both for the user and researcher, but there are a lot of places in the pipeline where errors could be propagated, and there didn't seem to be much analysis surrounding that. The assessment of the open vocabulary systems was essentially always on closed vocabulary sets, so the benefits of it can't really be compared well against the penalties. My guess is that as more vocabulary is used, opportunity for ambiguity increases, which would continue to reduce performance after a certain point, but that point may be such a large vocabulary that it might not even matter. The biggest issue isn't my guess, but that there wasn't really an assessment to identify flaws and potentially open up different architectures. 
3. Soft robotics is still fun and creative. While many techniques have continued to develop, with best practices greatly improving quality, the applications keep growing. They are rarely the most practical of robots, but they sure are fun. I rarely regret stopping by a soft robotics section because of the creativity that seems to stem from them. That doesn't seem to have changed in 9 years, though the big question still is: can we actually use them for anything?
4. The floor is just a different place with so many humanoid, quadrupeds, and dexterous hands floating around. I can't tell you how many actual working demos on the floor there were 9 years ago, but it was nowhere close to what I saw this year. It certainly makes it lively, and as much as I am not a big believer in humanoid robots in the near term or possibly ever, watching them this past week, you could see how and why people are excited about them. 
5. Professor Ankur Mehta had a keynote on "Robotics problems that robots can't solve where he talked about how the general population's view of robots has shifted, and that would, in the long term, be a problem for researchers. This made me think about how the choice of what problems we try to solve eventually affects perception. As researchers, we often have ideas in search of problems. To write the paper or get the funding, we then slap on a vague explanation of what those ideas could eventually solve. When that happens, and we say the goal is to do something a human does now because of some long-term concern about population demographics, it certainly creates the impression that we are trying to replace people because we are saying as much. If you compare that to Professor Brittany Duncan's work that immediately followed, where most of the projects she presented were of her lab working with environmental scientists of one type or another to do things they otherwise would not be able to, monitor beavers, controlled burns, or study the rainforest that's not some vague threat to humans because we are using robots to do something that we can't do today. I think Professor Mehta was right to raise that concern, but I think Professor Duncan followed up with the answer.
6. My last thought is how much my perspective has changed. Nine years ago, I had just graduated with my PhD, and conferences were where you showed how much you'd progressed the field, networked, had a few too many drinks, and learned how others were progressing the field. Now, when I walk through the poster sessions, I find myself thinking that the biggest value is the quality of education that results from doing research and sharing it with others. More than anything else that screamed to me as I walked around, my research questions and industry needs weren't frequently being addressed, but I could see students growing into the people who would eventually answer them, and that was awesome.

Overall, it was a lot of fun to come back to IROS after so much time. I learned a lot and saw some techniques and approaches I am going to steal (appropriately cited, of course) for my problems and research.
